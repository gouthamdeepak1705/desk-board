# Make Desk Board open apps by voice

**Recommendation: build this in layers, and do not start with a "screenshot-and-click" AI agent.** Most of what you want to say to the board ("open Chrome and search for X", "open Notepad", "type this", "switch to Spotify") needs no AI vision at all. It can be done by giving your existing OpenAI Realtime voice assistant a few plain, safe tools (launch an app, open a web address, press a shortcut, type text). That is fast (about 1 to 3 seconds), nearly free, and reliable. The full "AI looks at the screen and clicks" approach, called computer use, is real and good enough for short tasks. The best agents now beat the human baseline on a standard short-task test (OSWorld-Verified, human score 72.4%, top boards around 86 to 90%) ([Presenc AI](https://presenc.ai/research/osworld-computer-use-leaderboard-2026)). But it is slow (tens of seconds to minutes), costs cents per task, fails more often than benchmarks suggest on long jobs, and can be tricked by text on a web page. So add it second, as a background helper behind a stop button and approval prompts. Voice makes the risks worse, because a misheard word can trigger a real action. The plan below ranks each step by cost, delay and risk, and says which parts are proven and which are experimental. Note that the AI model names in the sources (Fable, Mythos, Astra, Sol) came from vendor docs and leaderboard sites I could not cross-check, so treat exact model choices as "verify before you commit".

## Computer use means an AI that looks at your screen and presses keys

"Computer use" is a loop. The program takes a screenshot, sends it to an AI model, and the model replies with one action such as "click at this spot", "type this", "press Ctrl+L" or "scroll down". The program carries out that action, takes a new screenshot, and repeats until the model says it is done ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool)). The important point for you is that **the AI never touches your PC directly**. With Anthropic, OpenAI and Google alike, your own code takes the screenshots and performs the clicks, so Desk Board would have to supply both ([OpenAI guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-computer-use), [Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/computer-use)). The consumer products (Claude Cowork, ChatGPT, Chrome's Gemini features) do not offer a way to drive your own app.

Two technical details cause most real-world bugs. First, the model reasons in the pixels of the picture you send, so you must shrink screenshots to a size it accepts (Anthropic suggests 1280x720 or 1024x768, and rejects oversize images) and convert its coordinates back to real screen positions ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool)). Windows display scaling (125 to 150%) and multiple monitors make this easy to get wrong. That is my inference, since Anthropic only documents the Mac case. Second, each screenshot costs roughly 1,000 to 1,800 tokens, so a long task piles up cost and slows down unless old images are pruned ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool)).

There is a second way to "see" the screen that is not pictures. Windows exposes a structured list of buttons, text boxes and menus to accessibility tools, called UI Automation (UIA). Agents that read this list are faster and more exact where it works, but it is patchy for games, remote desktops and custom-drawn app content. Current advice from tool builders is to use a mix: shortcuts and direct commands first, the accessibility list second, screenshots last ([t8r.tech](https://t8r.tech/alternative/compare/accessibility-tree-vs-screenshot-automation), [LUMOS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.30697)). I found no head-to-head Windows benchmark proving the speed gain, so treat the size of the advantage as unmeasured.

## Short tasks work today, long unattended jobs do not

The benchmark picture needs careful reading. On OSWorld-Verified (369 short tasks in a clean virtual machine), the top entries claim about 86 to 90%, above the 72.4% human baseline ([Presenc AI](https://presenc.ai/research/osworld-computer-use-leaderboard-2026)). I could only see these through aggregator sites, not the official board. On OSWorld 2.0 (108 long workflows, around 318 tool calls each), the best official result is only **about 44% fully completed** for Claude Opus 5, and Anthropic itself reports about 49% for a newer model ([Presenc AI](https://presenc.ai/research/osworld-computer-use-leaderboard-2026)). Scores also move a lot with benchmark version changes alone (one model went from 31% to 44% with no model change), so do not trust single numbers.

Repeatability is the bigger worry. An April 2026 academic study found that success depends heavily on random variation between runs, and recommends testing each task many times ([arXiv 2604.17849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17849)). A secondary article cites a strong agent that succeeded 78% of the time per attempt but only 36% across all ten attempts; I could not verify that original ([Harcourt Substack](https://marissaharcourt.substack.com/p/computer-using-agents-are-production)). Simple arithmetic points the same way: at 98% accuracy per step, a 5-step task works about 90% of the time, a 20-step task 67%, and a 50-step task 36% ([Contra Collective](https://contracollective.com/blog/computer-use-agents-production-reliability-cost-2026)). The commonly reported failures are saying "done" when it is not, repeating the same action in a loop, misreading small text, and acting on a half-loaded page ([ERPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17885), [Harcourt Substack](https://marissaharcourt.substack.com/p/computer-using-agents-are-production)).

Speed is the other limit. An independent study found the best agents take 2.7 to 4.3 times more steps than a human would, and later steps run slower as context grows ([OSWorld-Human](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16042)). Anthropic's own docs say the latency is "too slow for human-speed interaction". Blog estimates of 2 to 5 seconds per step are unbenchmarked. My rough estimate (not measured) is 15 to 45 seconds for "open the browser and search" done purely by screenshots, against about 1 to 3 seconds by direct commands. **Reliable today: short 1 to 10 step tasks with a checkable result. Experimental: anything long, multi-app, or unattended.**

| OpenAI Responses API computer tool | Same pattern; gpt-6.1-sol at $2/$10 ([docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-computer-use), [pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)) | Supported, no maturity label seen | Good, and it keeps you on one vendor |

| Windows-MCP (open source, MIT) | Local server giving an AI 21 tools: click, type, shortcut, launch app, UI snapshot | Very active, about 10,400 GitHub stars ([GitHub](https://github.com/CursorTouch/Windows-MCP)) | Best open-source fit, with caveats below |
| UFO (Microsoft), UI-TARS Desktop, Agent S | Standalone research-style agents | Active, heavier | Harder to wire into Electron |
| Playwright MCP, browser-use | Browser-only automation | Active ([GitHub](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright-mcp)) | Good for web tasks only |

Microsoft's side is mostly a closed door for a hobby app: the agent workspace needs an Insider build and an administrator switch, and Copilot Studio's computer use is an enterprise cloud product ([Microsoft](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/ai/ai-features/experimental-agentic-features)). I found no official general-availability announcement. For tools, avoid nut.js (its free prebuilt packages were removed) and pyautogui (no updates since August 2024) ([nut.js](https://github.com/nut-tree/nut.js)). Windows-MCP needs Python 3.14 or newer via `uv`, sends anonymous telemetry unless you turn it off, and exposes PowerShell, Registry and file tools. Versions before 0.7.5 had a bug that allowed unauthenticated PowerShell execution in some HTTP modes ([release alert](https://releasealert.dev/github/CursorTouch/Windows-MCP/cves)). If you use it, pin a recent version, use stdio only, and block the dangerous tools. I found no independent reliability test of it, and nothing on how well it reads Electron app interfaces.

## Voice plus computer use is a fast talker with a slow worker

Your setup already matches the pattern the industry settled on: a realtime voice model talks instantly and hands slow jobs to a background agent. OpenAI shipped exactly this in ChatGPT Voice on desktop on 2026-07-23, reportedly with the caveat that misheard commands on autonomous actions are costly ([secondary summary](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/8825/chatgpt-voice-desktop-control-ai-agents); I did not find OpenAI's own post). I found no mature open-source "voice plus Windows computer use" project, only voice layers over coding agents like [Duck Talk](https://github.com/dhuynh95/duck_talk). So you would be building something new, not installing it.

The recommended design has three speeds. **Fast lane (about 1 to 3 seconds):** the realtime model calls small tools directly, such as `open_app`, `open_url` (the search is just a Google address with your words in it), `press_shortcut` and `type_text`. "Open Chrome and search for cats" becomes one `open_url` call and needs no screenshots. **Middle lane (a few seconds per step):** a background agent using the Windows accessibility list through Windows-MCP, run by your Claude Code backend or the Anthropic API. **Slow lane (15 seconds to minutes):** screenshot-based clicking, only when the other lanes fail. The realtime model gets a `computer_task(goal)` tool that returns at once with a task ID, plus `computer_status` and `computer_cancel`. The GA Realtime API lets the conversation continue while a tool call is pending, and adds placeholder replies so the model can say it is still waiting ([OpenAI notes](https://developers.openai.com/blog/realtime-api)). The documented catch is that the model sometimes invents a result that has not arrived, so instruct it never to claim success before a real result ([OpenAI notes](https://developers.openai.com/blog/realtime-api)). OpenAI's docs do not say what happens to a pending tool call when you interrupt by speaking, so test that on gpt-realtime-2.1; I would also make "stop" a local keyword and a hotkey that kill the agent regardless of the model.

Keep the agent loop in the Electron main process or a child process, not the window that holds the WebRTC voice connection, so a CPU spike does not glitch audio. Do not send screenshots into the voice session; send one-line summaries. Three Windows 11 traps matter. Windows silently ignores simulated input aimed at admin-level windows (UIPI), so tell the user "that window is elevated" instead of failing quietly ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/winuser/nf-winuser-sendinput)). Windows restricts which program may take focus, so launching apps with a process start is more reliable than clicking ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/winuser/nf-winuser-setforegroundwindow)). And your wallpaper-layer window is an unusual case: it is not a normal clickable window, and I found no source on how screen capture or input behave around it. The agent should treat the board as background and test this by hand early. Whether the wallpaper trick works on your Windows build 26300 is also unverified.

## Prompt injection and misheard words are the real dangers

The top risk is that **text on a page or screen can give the AI orders**. This is called prompt injection, and it has been demonstrated against Claude Computer Use, Comet, Fellou, Opera Neon and ChatGPT Atlas, including hidden or faint text inside images that a screenshot reader obeys ([Brave](https://brave.com/blog/unseeable-prompt-injections/)). Anthropic says plainly that it is "far from a solved problem" and measures roughly 1.4 to 2.4% attack success with its strongest defenses, versus 8 to 14% for the models alone, in its own tests ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses)). An independent test found a strong attacker succeeded 63% of the time with 100 tries against one model ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/claude-opus-4-5-resists-prompt-injections-better-than-rivals-but-still-falls-to-strong-attacks-alarmingly-often/), secondary). A hobby app lacks the vendors' extra classifiers, so expect nearer the unprotected numbers. Simon Willison's rule of thumb is to avoid combining private data, untrusted content and a way to send things out ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/)). A PC agent that browses the web, sees your files and can run commands has all three.

Voice adds its own risks: TV ads have triggered smart speakers before, researchers have shown near-inaudible audio can command audio AI models ([ChannelNews](https://www.channelnews.com.au/hidden-audio-attacks-could-turn-ai-assistants-into-security-risks/), secondary), and speech recognition mishears names and numbers. The mitigations below are my synthesis of the sources, not a tested recipe.

| Risk | Mitigation | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Injected page text | Domain allowlist; dedicated browser profile with no saved logins; no shell tools; agent sees no password manager or banking windows | Medium |
| Costly actions (send, delete, buy, install, settings) | Code-enforced approval card showing the exact action; confirm with a click or keypress, not voice | Low |
| Runaway agent | Global kill hotkey, local "stop" keyword, step cap (about 25), time limit, repeat-action detector | Low |
| Misheard or spoofed voice | Push-to-talk by default; show the transcript; mute while the board is speaking; no always-on listening for actions | Low |
| Leaked data | Screenshots go to the cloud; OpenAI keeps API logs up to 30 days ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data/index.html), secondary); Anthropic's retention wording varies by page, so check the live docs; keep API keys in the main process with Electron safeStorage and set provider spending caps | Low |
| Agent damage | Run non-elevated; prefer Recycle Bin over delete; log every action locally | Low |

A separate Windows account is the practical isolation step. I believe Windows Sandbox is not available on Windows 11 Home (your edition), but the sources did not confirm that, so verify before relying on it. Microsoft's own agent workspace uses a separate account and session for the same reason ([Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2025/10/16/securing-ai-agents-on-windows/)).

## A four-phase build, cheapest and safest first

Costs below are my arithmetic from published prices, not measurements. The voice session itself runs roughly $0.06 to $0.11 per minute on the flagship realtime model with caching ([Forasoft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/openai-realtime-api-pricing), secondary), which likely exceeds the cost of short agent tasks. A 10-step screenshot task on Sonnet 5.5 is roughly $0.03 to $0.25 depending on caching and on Haiku 5.5 about a cent; a 30-step task costs several times more because context grows each step, so prune old screenshots.

| Phase | What you build | Latency | Cost per use | Reliability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Direct tools (days) | `open_app`, `open_url`, `press_shortcut`, `type_text`, `focus_window`, `volume`; push-to-talk; stop hotkey; action log | 1 to 3 s | Near zero | Proven; works for "open Chrome and search for X" |
| 2. Approval and safety layer (days) | Risk tiers, approval card with exact action, URL allowlist, dedicated browser profile | Adds a click | None | Proven design, your code |
| 3. Structured agent (1 to 2 weeks) | `computer_task` async tool; Claude via your backend or API drives Windows-MCP (accessibility list first), limited tools, progress sent back to voice | 5 to 60 s | Cents | Works for short tasks; test heavily; some Electron apps may be unreadable |
| 4. Screenshot fallback (experimental) | Screenshot loop with correct scaling, Haiku first and Sonnet on failure, browser profile only, hard caps | 15 s to minutes | 1 to 30 cents | Experimental; expect failures and loops |

Do Phase 1 and 2 first and live with them for a while; they probably cover most of what you will actually ask for. Before Phase 3, run your own ten common tasks ten times each and record the pass rate, since the research shows single-run success misleads. Skip Microsoft's agent workspace for now, and consider registering Desk Board as a Windows MCP connector later only if it leaves preview.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which computer-use AI should I plug in?" to "how little AI do I need?" For spoken commands, the fastest and safest agent is often a short list of well-defined tools, with vision-based clicking reserved for the leftover cases. Benchmarks say the technology has crossed the human line on short tasks, but your experience will be shaped by the three things benchmarks hide: seconds-per-step latency, run-to-run inconsistency, and text on the screen that tries to give orders.

Several things remain unknown and need hands-on tests: how pending tool calls behave when you interrupt gpt-realtime-2.1, how screen capture and input behave next to a wallpaper-layer window, whether Windows-MCP reads your target apps well, and the real per-step speed on your machine. Prices and product status also moved quickly in 2026 (OpenAI's Atlas browser was reportedly retired in August, and some Gemini prices rise in December), so re-check vendor pages before you commit.
