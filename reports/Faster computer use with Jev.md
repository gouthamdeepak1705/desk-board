# Jev helps at the edges, not the core

**Verdict: Jev will not be the big speed-up you are hoping for, but it is a cheap, useful helper, and the real gains come from avoiding screenshots altogether.** Jev is a fast "classifier" (it picks from answers you define and returns confidence scores; it cannot write text, plan or click), so it can sit in a few spots in Desk Board for roughly 0.1 to 0.3 seconds a call and a tiny fraction of a cent. It cannot replace the slow part of your screenshot agent, which is the big model thinking and re-checking, not clicking. The new image input, bounding box ("box") and long-context abilities are real (version 1.14, released 2026-10-14) but TypeSafe itself labels them experimental and not for production, and no one has published a test of them on computer screens. The fastest path to "state of the art" is a ladder of lanes: voice rules and direct tools first, Windows accessibility data (UI Automation, "UIA") and web-page structure second, saved replays of tasks you repeat third, and the 5 to 10 second screenshot loop only as a last resort. Build that first and measure it; add Jev later as a small router, success-checker and safety gate. The speed-up numbers below are my estimates unless a source is cited, and several vendor figures are unverified.

## What Jev is, and what its new abilities really are

Jev is what TypeSafe calls a "System One" model: you send it a description of a situation plus multiple-choice, score or yes/no questions, and it returns typed answers with probabilities in one pass. It "do[es] not write replies, produce code, or generate explanations of their reasoning" ([TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one.md)). Think of it as a very fast, prompt-defined sorter rather than a chatbot. Pricing is **$0.042 per million input tokens, with output free** ([docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)), so cost is a non-issue. The vendor claims 70 to 500 ms per call ([typesafe.ai](https://typesafe.ai)). Independent-ish data is more modest: an analysis of launch-week posts found a median reported latency of about 76 ms and an upper quartile of 270 ms, and a median speedup of 7x, not the 193.6x headline ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/typesafe-ai-jev-released)). Critics also note the comparison is unfair because Jev skips writing an answer. Plan on roughly 100 to 300 ms including network (my planning figure).

Your claims about new abilities hold up with caveats. Version 1.14 adds image input (PNG or JPEG, 280 to 1568 pixels per side), a `box` question that returns one rectangle as fractions of the image plus a confidence, and an opt-in 262,000-token context that is slower and less accurate than the default 64,000 ([docs: images](https://docs.typesafe.ai/images), [docs: box](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/box), [docs: models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)). Images need a special "danger zone" header, and TypeSafe says the model does worse on images than on text and advises against production use ([Pillitteri](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/26048/jev-1-14-image-input-box-typesafe-warns), a secondary report). "Ability to choose" is not new; multiple-choice questions existed at launch ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/typesafe-ai-jev-released)). The docs disagree with each other on whether images are supported at all, and I could not read TypeSafe's own release post, so check the live docs.

The box feature has limits that matter for clicking. It always returns a rectangle even when the target is not on screen, returns only one, can miss small targets in big images, has no recommended confidence cut-off, and gives a box, not a click point ([docs: box](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/box)). A 4K Windows screen would have to be shrunk or cropped to fit under 1568 pixels, which hurts small buttons. No one has published a GUI-grounding benchmark for it (searches on 2026-10-26 found none). The only evidence for screens is builder self-reports, such as roughly 90 ms per decision when local text recognition feeds Jev text ([madewithjev](https://madewithjev.com/jev-images)), which is not a controlled test. TypeSafe's own list of weaknesses also bites here: counting, dates, big noisy inputs full of irrelevant content, a bias toward the first listed option, and not treating hostile text as hostile ([docs: jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13)). Its confidence is not a truth check, since it can be confidently wrong. On TypeSafe's own four-workflow test it agreed with reference answers 67.8% of the time, about the same as a mid-tier GPT model (67.9%) and below the bigger ones (73 to 74%), and those tests were written by TypeSafe and not reproduced independently ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/system-one-models-jev)). I could not confirm whether access is still waitlisted; the docs describe self-serve keys.

## Why the screenshot loop is slow, and why a faster clicker barely helps

The key finding is where the time goes. A study of 16 agents measured one agent (Agent S2) and found planning took 49 to 59% of the time and reflection (the "did that work?" re-check) 15 to 45%, together **75 to 94%**, while finding where to click ("grounding") took only **3 to 6%** and taking screenshots and performing actions 1 to 4% each ([OSWorld-Human](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.16042v1)). Agents also took 1.4 to 4.3 times more steps than a human (the paper's versions disagree on the range), and later steps got slower as context piled up ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16042)). That is one 2025 agent on Linux, so treat the exact split as a pattern, not a measurement of your machine. The implication still holds: Jev's box feature attacks the 3 to 6% slice. Even if it were perfect and instant, your 5 to 10 seconds per step would barely move. What shrinks that time is fewer model calls, lower reasoning effort, smaller context, and not using vision at all when something cheaper works.

Running a vision model locally is not the answer on your laptop either. The small specialist screen models (Holo2-4B and 8B, Fara1.5-4B and 9B, GUI-Actor-3B, UI-TARS-1.5-7B) score roughly 40 to 59% on the hard ScreenSpot-Pro grounding test, whereas aggregator sites list top cloud models at 84 to 88% (aggregators, unverified) ([Holo2-8B card](https://huggingface.co/Hcompany/Holo2-8B), [BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/screenspot-pro)). I found no measured speed for any of them on a Ryzen 6600H integrated graphics chip. My guess, to be tested rather than assumed, is that a 7B model chewing a screenshot on that chip takes many seconds, no faster than your cloud call. Microsoft's OmniParser (a screen parser) is quoted at 0.6 s a frame on a datacenter A100 GPU, and about 3 to 6 s on a hosted T4 ([Hugging Face card](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/OmniParser-v2.0/blob/refs%2Fpr%2F21/README.md), [Replicate](https://replicate.com/microsoft/omniparser-v2)). Skip local vision for now. Keep your gpt-6.1-sol loop as the fallback and make it rarer.

## Build a lane ladder so most requests never see a screenshot

The biggest speed-up is to match each request to the cheapest lane that can do it, and move down the ladder only when a lane's result check fails. The router notes call this a cascade and note it suits desktop work because actions have cheap checks (the window exists, the page loaded, the file was written). The per-lane timings below are my estimates, except the UIA figure, which is a project's own claim rather than a benchmark.

| Lane | Handles | Typical time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Rules | "volume up", "open X", "go to Y", "play/pause" | under 10 ms to decide | Plain pattern matching on the transcript |
| 1. Direct tools | open_app, open_url, press_shortcut, type_text, focus_window, media keys | about 1 s (you already measure this) | Deterministic |
| 2. Connectors/APIs | Calendar, mail, files, chat | about 0.3 to 2 s | Only where a connector exists and works; not verified |
| 3. Windows UIA agent | Native apps with readable controls | a few seconds per step | Windows-MCP lists 0.2 to 0.9 s between actions before model time ([Claude connectors listing](https://claude.com/connectors/windows-mcp)); the study above found building an accessibility tree took 3 to 26 s per step on Linux, so read only the active window |
| 4. Browser structure | Web pages | similar to lane 3 | Playwright's default mode reads page structure, not pixels ([Playwright](https://playwright.dev/mcp/snapshots)) |
| 5. Screenshot vision | Anything else: games, custom-drawn apps, remote desktops | 5 to 10 s per step (yours) | Last resort |
| 6. Human handoff | Logins, CAPTCHAs, payments, admin prompts | n/a | Say so out loud and stop |

A simple rule for picking: if the request names a known app or address, use lane 1; a service with a connector, lane 2; a native Windows app, lane 3; a web page, lane 4; and everything else, lane 5. Windows has two traps that make lane 1 and 3 actions silently fail: input to an administrator-level window is blocked with no error ([Microsoft SendInput](https://learn.microsoft.com/tr-tr/windows/win32/api/winuser/nf-winuser-sendinput)), and UIA rectangles are in physical pixels while some cursor calls use scaled ones ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-ca/%20dotnet/framework/ui-automation/ui-automation-and-screen-scaling)). So verify results by reading real state (window list, address bar, file exists) rather than trusting a "success" return.

Three more speed levers apply inside the slow lanes. First, batching: OpenAI's computer tool returns several actions at once, and the guide advises one screenshot after a short group ([OpenAI guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-computer-use)); Microsoft's UFO2 agent reports up to 51% fewer model calls from predicting several actions at a time, but that figure comes from a third-party page ([summary](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/ufo2-the-desktop-agentos)). Batch only when the next screen is predictable, such as typing into a field you just focused. Second, set the reasoning effort to low, as OpenAI's own example does, and keep screenshots near 1280x720 instead of full 3440x1440 ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool)). Third, split planner and executor: one stronger call writes a plan with the keyboard shortcuts it already knows, a cheaper step runs it, and a check happens at the end. I found no measured saving for this split, so treat it as an experiment.

## Replay what worked, and use Jev where its strengths fit

Repeated tasks are your best "speed-up for free." A June 2026 preprint (PreAct, a single-author paper, so vendor-of-its-own-results) saves the first successful run as a step-by-step program that checks the screen matches before each step and hands back to the AI if not, reporting replays **8.5 to 13 times faster** with no model calls per step ([arXiv 2606.17929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17929)). Browser tools Stagehand and Skyvern do the same for web tasks ([Stagehand](https://docs.stagehand.dev/best-practices/deterministic-agent), [Skyvern](https://docs-new.skyvern.com/docs/features/code-caching)). The documented risk is stale values replayed blindly, so store the task with blanks (the search term, the file name) and check every step. A separate study found that showing an agent demonstrations improved success, time and cost ([SCUBA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26506)). Only save a replay after one clean, verified run.

Jev earns a place in four narrow jobs, all fed with text rather than raw screenshots. The first is a cheap second opinion for lane choice on ambiguous requests. Note that the Realtime model already picks tools, so Jev would add 100 to 300 ms to commands that rules can handle in milliseconds; use it only for the leftovers. The second is picking among a short list of controls you already have from UIA ("which of these 8 buttons matches the goal?"), with the order shuffled to dodge first-option bias. The third is cheap yes/no checks on text from the before and after screens: page loaded, dialog present, error shown, action succeeded. Several can be batched into one call. The fourth is a safety gate for your three hard rules (spending money, permanent deletion, passwords): classify each proposed action as "needs approval" or "ordinary" so everything else runs without asking. A community tool already screens agent shell commands with Jev at about 250 ms ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/typesafe-ai-jev-released), secondary). Do not rely on Jev alone for this gate, because it can be confidently wrong, and keep simple code rules (a list of money, delete and password words and windows) as the first line. Screenshot or web text is untrusted and can steer Jev, which is the injection risk I flagged in the earlier research.

A related product, Jev Router on OpenRouter, picks which model and reasoning effort to use per text request and is billed at the chosen model's price ([OpenRouter docs](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/routing/routers/jev-router.md)). It might help your Claude Code or text-agent lanes, but it publishes no latency or price overhead and its claimed 82% win over OpenRouter's Auto router comes from a low-quality news item, so treat it as unproven.

## Make voice feel instant and never block on slow work

Voice delay is the other half of "fast." The levers are documented but unmeasured for gpt-realtime-2.1. Semantic voice detection has an "eagerness" setting (high ends turns sooner), and the API reference warns it can add delay compared with a simple timeout ([OpenAI VAD guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad)). Try eagerness high or a short silence timer and check for cut-offs. Use minimal or low reasoning effort and keep the tool list short, one entry per lane, since a long tool list hurts speed and choice (community figures put single browser tool servers at 13,000 to 19,000 tokens of definitions ([Mintlify](https://mintlify.com/shanraisshan/claude-code-best-practice/reports/browser-automation-mcp))). The mini model is cheaper and faster but should be A/B tested on your real commands ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/techsignaldaily/building-low-latency-voice-agents-with-openais-gpt-realtime-21-and-gpt-realtime-21-mini-3mnj), secondary).

For anything over about two seconds, return at once with "started" and a job number, let the voice say "on it," and report back later; the model otherwise stays silent while a tool runs, and forum users say spoken lead-ins are inconsistent ([MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/build-production-voice-agent-gpt-realtime-2-api-setup), [OpenAI forum](https://community.openai.com/t/realtime-api-preamble-inconsistent/1361953)). So also play a short local sound or chime from your own code. A fast path, my own proposal not a documented feature: if the live transcript matches a rule with high confidence, run the action immediately and tell the model afterward, with a duplicate-action guard so the model's own call does not run it twice. Pre-warm the browser, the UIA helper and connectors at app start. One forum user reported latency climbing to about a minute in long sessions with noise reduction and semantic detection on ([OpenAI forum](https://community.openai.com/t/realtime-api-with-noise-reduction-has-sudden-increase-of-latency/1256390), anecdotal), so log timing by session age and restart the session if it creeps up.

Your "stop asking permission" wish is achievable by making permission a code rule rather than a model habit: ordinary actions run, and only money, permanent delete and passwords raise a prompt, with a log of everything and a stop hotkey. Keep the earlier advice on running non-elevated, preferring the Recycle Bin and a dedicated browser profile. A report that gpt-6.1-sol tried to work around explicit restrictions in 23.5% of adversarial safety tests ([ProPakistani](https://propakistani.pk/2026/09/30/gpt-6-1-sol-brings-astra-level-performance-at-5x-lower-cost/), secondary) is a reason to enforce limits in code, not just in the prompt.

## Ranked build list and phased plan

This ranking weighs speed gain, effort and risk. Speed gains are my estimates, not measurements.

| Rank | Build | Expected gain | Cost | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rules plus lane-oriented tools; async "started/done" jobs with spoken acknowledgement | Common commands drop from seconds to about 1 s; slow jobs stop blocking talk | Free | Low |
| 2 | Measurement harness: 50 to 100 real commands with automatic pass/fail checks and timings | Makes every other choice evidence-based | Free | Low |
| 3 | Code-based permission rules (money, delete, passwords only) and stop hotkey | Removes constant prompts | Free | Medium if rules are sloppy |
| 4 | UIA lane (Windows-MCP or your own, active window only) and browser-structure lane | Maybe 2 to 5 times faster per step than screenshots on supported apps (unmeasured) | Low | Medium: admin windows, Electron apps unverified |
| 5 | Replay cache for repeated tasks with per-step checks | Up to roughly 8 to 13 times faster on repeats (preprint claim) | Low | Medium: stale values |
| 6 | Tune the screenshot loop: lower effort, 1280x720, action batching, early exit | Cut calls per task, maybe 30 to 50% (UFO2 reports up to 51% fewer calls) | Low | Low |
| 7 | Jev as text router, step checker and safety gate; keep VLM fallback and threshold tests | Saves a few seconds on ambiguous or verify steps; cost near zero | A fraction of a cent per call | Medium: confident errors, injection |
| 8 | Jev images/box for clicking | Unknown | Unknown | High: experimental, no GUI benchmark |

Phase 1 (week 1) is items 1 to 3: rules, async jobs, the harness and permission rules, tested with a stopwatch on your own commands. Phase 2 (weeks 2 to 3) adds item 4 and the tuning in item 6, and the first replays. Phase 3 (week 4) adds Jev behind a switch, running in "shadow mode" where it answers but cannot act, so you can compare its answers with what actually happened on your logged commands before trusting it; calibrate the cut-offs on your own cases and pin `jev-1.14.0` rather than the moving "latest" label. Phase 4 is optional: spend an afternoon testing Jev box and a small local model on 30 screenshots of your real apps against your cloud model, and adopt either only if it wins on both accuracy and time.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question from "which model clicks fastest?" to "how rarely do I need to click at all?" Because model thinking and re-checking eat roughly three quarters or more of a screenshot step (in the one study that measured it), speeding up the click is the wrong target, and Jev, a clicker-adjacent tool at best, is better treated as inexpensive glue: a router, a checker and a gate that decides when to wake the slow model.

"Capable of anything" will still mean "fast on known paths, graceful hand-off elsewhere." Big benchmark scores do not transfer: one analysis cites a model dropping from 83.5% on OSWorld-Verified to 20.6% on the harder OSWorld 2.0 ([youngju.dev](https://www.youngju.dev/transcribe/2026-07-17-computer-use-agents-what-benchmarks-measure.en), secondary). Still unknown, and worth a real test: Jev's image and box accuracy and latency on Windows screens, whether UIA reads your Electron apps, how interruption affects pending tool calls, and Jev's current access terms.
